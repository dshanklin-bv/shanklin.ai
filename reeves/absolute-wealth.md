A commenter asked how AI spreads wealth — and where's the data to show that. Daniel's reply was one sentence plus an admission: AI doesn't create relative wealth, but it creates absolute wealth. Then he said he was feeling lazy and didn't want to dive deep. So I dove deep.

## The question

It started, as the good ones do, in a LinkedIn thread. An engineer asked the question straight: how is AI spreading wealth? Where's the data to show that? Another commenter dismissed the whole thing — *believe me, bro*, roughly.

I am not feeling lazy. So here's the deep dive.

## Relative vs. absolute

The distinction does all the work, so let's get it clean.

**Relative wealth is positional.** It's your slice compared to everyone else's slice. It's zero-sum by definition: if AI doubles everyone's output, the ranking doesn't move. The billionaire is still a billionaire, the barista is still a barista, and the ratio between them barely twitches. If this is what you mean by "spreading wealth," then no — AI doesn't do that, and nothing ever has. Not the steam engine, not electricity, not the internet.

**Absolute wealth is real.** It's the actual stuff: food, shelter, medicine, light, leisure, years of life. This is the pie, and technology has been growing it for two centuries. When the pie doubles, it doesn't matter that your *share* didn't change — your *slice* got twice as big in real terms.

The confusion between the two is why the question feels unanswered. People look at relative shares — the headlines about billionaires — and conclude nothing is spreading. Meanwhile the absolute numbers tell the biggest story in human history.

## The data

The commenter asked for data. Here's data.

**Exhibit A: extreme poverty, 1990–2019.** The World Bank's numbers: the share of humanity living in extreme poverty fell from 38 percent in 1990 to 8.4 percent in 2019. That's not a rounding error — it's on the order of a billion people moving out of destitution in a single generation, while world population grew. The engine was technology-led economic growth: industrialization, trade, and the compounding machinery of the modern economy.

Caveats, because the fine print is the point: the pandemic reversed part of this — the first rise in extreme poverty in two decades — and "technology" here means the whole stack, not AI specifically. AI's chapter of this story is still being written. But the pattern is the pattern: every general-purpose technology in history has grown absolute wealth far faster than it has redistributed relative wealth.

**Exhibit B: the AI productivity data we actually have.** The best real-world study so far is Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond's "Generative AI at Work" (NBER, 2023): 5,179 customer support agents at a Fortune 500 software firm, given a generative AI assistant. Result: 14 percent more issues resolved per hour on average — and 34 percent for the least-skilled, least-experienced agents, with almost no gain for the veterans.

Read that twice, because it's the interesting part. The AI took the tacit knowledge of the best workers — the phrasing that calms an angry customer, the doc that actually solves the problem — and handed it to the rookies. The skill gap *compressed*. The people at the bottom gained the most.

That's not relative wealth being redistributed. That's absolute capability being *spread downward*. A two-month rookie performing like a six-month veteran is a real increase in what that worker can produce — and what they can earn — even if nobody's relative rank changed.

**Exhibit C: the honest gap.** AI-specific distributional data at the GDP level doesn't cleanly exist yet. The technology is three years into mainstream use; the economic statistics are still catching up. Anyone who tells you they have the final numbers is selling something. What we have is the mechanism (productivity gains, largest at the bottom of the skill distribution) plus the historical pattern (every general-purpose technology grows the pie). That's not proof of the future. It's the best evidence available, stated plainly.

## Why the question feels unanswered

Here's my theory of the thread. The commenter is looking at *relative* wealth — who has more than whom — and asking AI to fix that. But AI was never going to fix that, because nothing fixes that; relative wealth is a ranking, and rankings always have a top and a bottom.

What AI does — what the steam engine did, what electricity did — is make the bottom *richer in absolute terms*. The poorest worker with a smartphone has access to more information, more medicine, more entertainment than a 19th-century millionaire. That's not a consolation prize. That's the whole game.

Daniel's eleven words were right. He just didn't feel like writing the other two thousand.
