## The evening of "kill all"

That evening worked because the three days before it had built the machine that made it possible. Here's how the machine gets built.

## The $16.99 detective story

September 19. A $16.99 video-conferencing charge posts, and it becomes target number one. This is the worked example — the one kill that taught us the pattern for all the rest.

First, the trace: the account identity is followed through a Google SSO login to the vendor's own billing portal. No support ticket, no chat queue — the cancel button inside the vendor's system, hit directly. Then the wait. Eight days later the confirmation email lands: service runs through October 19, then drops to the free tier. End of story, except it wasn't the end — it was the template. Cancel inside the billing system. Collect the confirmation. Record the end-of-service date. Every kill after that one ran the same three moves.

> The $16.99 kill taught the pattern. The pattern killed the other eight.

## The method, earned

Five steps, in order, every time. Skip a step and the number is a rumor.

*Fig. 01 — the lifecycle (diagram on the rich page): inventory → evidence pack → schedule against renewals → verify like an auditor → scoreboard.*

1. **Inventory.** Pull every recurring charge from 90 days of bank and card statements — not the ones you remember, the ones the statements show. On the morning of September 22, this surfaced the forgotten ones: a streaming plan nobody watched, a camera add-on, an annual renewal converting silently. The money was never in the bills Daniel remembered.
2. **Evidence pack.** Every charge gets the full treatment before it counts: merchant, amount, frequency, account identity, receipt with date and reference, matched card transaction, next renewal date. *No pack, no kill.*
3. **Schedule against renewal dates.** A cancellation that lands after the charge posts is a failure with extra steps. Work in deadline order.
4. **Verify like an auditor.** Confirmation reference, end-of-service date, proof the charge stopped. The $11.94 headless-browser downgrade only moved from "claimed" to "proven" when the vendor's own refund email arrived on October 2.
5. **Scoreboard.** Only proven kills count. Planned, attempted, and "probably dead" do not. This discipline is the entire difference between $10/month and $500/month.

## The one that almost got away

Here's the stakes, honestly stated. There's a $300/month platform charge under investigation right now — a different $300 from the canceled AI browser tool, which is dead and counted; this one's separate and unproven. If it proves recurring, the pretty picture above flips until it's dealt with — which is exactly why the scoreboard only counts proven kills. The method doesn't promise you'll like every number. It promises every number is true.

And the counterweight: on September 25, a design tool survived. Instead of a kill it got reclassified as a business expense — because not everything recurring is waste, and the method says prove it either way before it counts. Judgment, not just killing.

## What to actually type

You don't need special phrasing. Plain instructions, in order:

> **The three prompts that start it:**
> "List every recurring charge in my last 90 days of statements: merchant, amount, frequency, account billed."
> "For each charge, assemble the evidence pack: account identity, receipt date and reference, matched card transaction, next renewal date."
> "Order the kill list by renewal date. Nothing gets canceled without my explicit approval first."

That last line is load-bearing. The machine prepares; you decide. Batch the approvals — review the list once, approve the batch, let it execute. Decisions batched, not drip-fed.

## What not to do

- **Don't automate the irreversible.** Every cancellation gets explicit approval. No exceptions, no "you know what I meant."
- **Protect the essentials.** Name what's never allowed to be touched — internet, phone number, anything that would ruin your week if it vanished.
- **Don't count pipeline kills.** Something you're "about to cancel" has saved exactly $0.

> **The one rule:** Skepticism is the correct default — aimed precisely. Check the machine's work the way you'd check an employee's: trust the system, verify the outputs, keep the receipts.
